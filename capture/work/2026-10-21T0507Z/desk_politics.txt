Throughout his three presidential campaigns, he claimed again and again to be the savior of working-class America. Those claims, however, mask his real agenda: to protect and enhance the wealth and income of the superrich — most importantly himself — while leaving driblets behind for the struggling working class.\n\nThe actual policies Trump has enacted, given the chance to make good on his promise to remember the forgotten, show that he has little or no regard for America’s forgotten men and women unless by forgotten he means himself.\n\nIf Trump felt any obligation to truth — ha, ha, ha — he would place himself in the same tradition as King Louis XIV of France, not in the line of Andrew Jackson and Bernie Sanders.\n\nIn reply to my inquiry about Trump’s regressive policies, Emmanuel Saez, an economist and director of the Stone Center on Wealth and Income Inequality at the University of California, Berkeley, wrote by email:\n\nOur real-time inequality data show that since the beginning of 2025, when Trump returned to the White House, incomes of the bottom 90 percent of Americans have stagnated after taking into account price inflation. Incomes of the top 10 percent have grown 6 percent with even higher growth for upper-income groups such as the top 1 percent.\n\nThe regressive redistribution of income is more striking when analyzed in terms of actual dollar changes.\n\nAdults in the bottom half of the income distribution experienced a modest loss of $31 dollars.\n\nThose in the middle gained $570 on average.\n\nIt’s a very different story for those in the top rungs. For the top 10 percent, a gain of $17,000; the top 1 percent, $110,000; top 0.1 percent, $520,000; top 0.01 percent, $2.8 million; top 0.001, $20 million each.\n\nEven more striking, Saez wrote, “has been the surge in the wealth of the billionaire class (top .001 percent, or top 2,600 richest American families) with wealth increases of about 18 percent since January 2025.”\n\nThe Federal Reserve has data comparing changes in net worth by income group.\n\nThe net worth of roughly 136,800 households in the top 0.1 percent grew to $27.87 trillion in the second quarter of 2026 from $22.27 trillion in the first quarter of 2025, a gain of $5.6 trillion.\n\nTrump and his family are leading this charge. Bush and Ronald Reagan, both no slouches when it comes to pushing through trickle-down legislation?\n\nI posed this question to Jason Furman, a chairman of the Council of Economic Advisers during the Obama administration and now a professor of economic policy at Harvard’s Kennedy School. In contrast President Bush actually slightly expanded all of those programs.\n\nPresident Reagan’s initial tax and spending policies were even more regressive than President Trump’s but the cumulative impact of his eight years was much less regressive largely because he signed multiple tax increases starting in 1982 that partly undid his huge 1981 tax cut and, even more important, he signed a series of Medicaid expansions starting in 1984 that were pushed by Henry Waxman and other Democrats.\n\nIn their June paper, “Bucking Backlash?
